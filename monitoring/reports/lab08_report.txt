Final Questions:

1. Why is observability different from simple logging?
Simple logging is just the passive emission of text records. 
Observability is a systemic property—the ability to understand the internal state of a complex system based purely on its external outputs 
by combining logs, metrics, and distributed traces into one unified context.

2. Why are metrics important in mission operations?
Metrics are computationally lightweight. They allow operators to monitor real-time trends (like CPU/Memory usage) 
and set up automated alerts without having to parse and search through terabytes of heavy log text.

3. Why are logs alone insufficient?
Logs lack cross-service context. In a highly distributed architecture, a single request might hit 10 microservices, 
making it impossible to follow the path using only text logs. 
Additionally, storing logs for every single microsecond of a space mission would overwhelm storage capacity.

4. What information do traces provide?
Traces show the exact lifecycle and path of a single request across multiple distributed nodes, 
explicitly measuring the latency (execution duration) at each specific step.

5. What is a span?
A span is the fundamental building block of a trace. It represents a single, logical unit of work (e.g., a database query or an HTTP response) 
and contains a start time, end time, and a unique Span ID.

6. How can OpenTelemetry correlate logs, metrics, and traces?
OpenTelemetry uses "Context Propagation". It injects a unique Trace ID into every request. 
This ID is then stamped onto all logs and metrics associated with that request, 
allowing an operator to click on an error log and instantly see the exact trace timeline.

7. How can observability help detect cyberattacks?
It exposes hidden anomalies. CPU metrics can reveal cryptomining malware or DoS attacks, 
while correlated logs can expose repeated brute-force administrative commands (like SHUTDOWN) coming from a single compromised account or IP.

8. Why is incident correlation important in distributed systems?
In distributed microservices, a failure in Service A is often just a symptom of Service C crashing. 
Correlation helps engineers instantly separate the root cause from the side effects, drastically reducing the mean time to recovery.
